Orthopedic surgery experts are among the most frequently retained medical experts in litigation, and for good reason. Orthopedic injuries and procedures show up across two large case types: medical malpractice claims against surgeons, and personal injury cases where the extent, cause, and future cost of an orthopedic injury is in dispute. The same specialty serves both, but the assignment is different. This guide covers when attorneys need an orthopedic surgery expert, what they testify to in each context, the credentials that matter, and how to match the right surgeon to your case.

## What Attorneys Are Actually Searching For

Attorneys look for orthopedic experts under several labels depending on the case. You might need an orthopedic malpractice expert to establish standard of care, an orthopedic injury expert to address causation and permanency in a PI case, a spine surgery expert for a back or neck claim, or a surgical error expert for a procedure that went wrong. All of these are orthopedic surgery experts, but the role differs by case type, and matching the surgeon's subspecialty to the body part and procedure at issue is what separates a strong expert from a weak one.

## When You Need an Orthopedic Surgery Expert Witness

There are two main contexts, and they call for the expert in different ways.

In medical malpractice cases, an orthopedic surgeon serves as a standard-of-care expert. They establish what a reasonably competent orthopedic surgeon should have done, and whether the defendant breached that standard. Common matters include surgical errors, misdiagnosis of fractures or other orthopedic conditions, nerve or vascular injury during surgery, hardware and implant complications, post-operative management failures, and infection. The expert addresses both breach of the standard of care and whether that breach caused the harm.

In personal injury cases, an orthopedic surgeon serves more often as a causation and damages expert. They address whether the accident caused the orthopedic injury claimed, the severity and permanency of the injury, the need for past and future surgery, work restrictions, and the reasonableness of the treatment provided. This is common in auto, trucking, premises, and workplace injury cases.

A single orthopedic expert can sometimes serve both roles, but in a med-mal case the standard-of-care function is primary, while in a PI case the causation and future-care function is primary.

## What an Orthopedic Surgery Expert Can Testify To

Depending on the case, common areas of opinion include:

Standard of care, meaning what a competent orthopedic surgeon should have done and whether the defendant met that standard. This is the core of an orthopedic malpractice case.

Causation, meaning whether the alleged negligence, or in a PI case the accident, caused the injury or outcome, as opposed to a pre-existing condition or the natural course of the disease.

Injury severity and permanency, including impairment ratings, functional limitations, and whether the injury is permanent.

Future treatment and surgery, including whether additional procedures will be needed and the basis for those projections, which often feeds the damages model.

Treatment reasonableness, meaning whether the care provided was appropriate and necessary, frequently disputed in PI damages.

Subspecialty alignment is essential here. Orthopedics covers spine, joint replacement, sports medicine, trauma, hand, foot and ankle, and more. A spine case needs a spine surgeon; a knee replacement case needs a surgeon who does knee replacements. The wrong subspecialty weakens the testimony and invites challenge.

## The Credentials That Matter

Board certification in orthopedic surgery is close to essential for testimony, along with subspecialty fellowship training where the case involves a subspecialty area like spine or hand surgery.

Active or recent clinical practice matters significantly. An expert who currently performs the procedure at issue is far stronger than one who retired from active surgery years ago, both for credibility and because many jurisdictions and standards require the expert to be in active practice or to have been at the relevant time.

Same-specialty matching is often a legal requirement, not just a best practice. Many states require that a standard-of-care expert in a medical malpractice case practice in the same specialty as the defendant. Confirm your jurisdiction's rule, because an otherwise excellent expert in the wrong specialty can be disqualified.

A testimony record matters as always. Ask about prior deposition and trial experience, any history of exclusion, and whether the surgeon testifies for both plaintiff and defense, which signals objectivity to a jury.

## Matching the Expert to Your Case Posture

In medical malpractice, plaintiff and defense both need a same-specialty orthopedic surgeon, one to establish breach and causation, the other to defend the care as within the standard. In personal injury, the plaintiff's orthopedic expert establishes that the accident caused a serious, possibly permanent injury requiring future care, while the defense expert often performs an independent medical examination and argues the injury is less severe, pre-existing, or unrelated to the accident. Surgeons who testify on both sides bring useful credibility.

Timing matters because the medical record is the foundation, and in PI cases the defense IME and the plaintiff's expert review need to be coordinated with case deadlines. In med-mal, the same-specialty requirement narrows the pool, so starting early is important to find a qualified, conflict-free expert who is genuinely in the right subspecialty.

## Common Questions Attorneys Ask

### When do I need an orthopedic surgery expert witness?

In two main situations: a medical malpractice case involving orthopedic care, where the surgeon establishes standard of care and causation, and a personal injury case, where the surgeon addresses whether the accident caused the injury, its severity and permanency, and future treatment needs. The role differs by case type, but the specialty serves both.

### Does my orthopedic expert have to be in the same subspecialty?

Subspecialty matching is critical for credibility, and in medical malpractice it is often a legal requirement. A spine case should have a spine surgeon, a joint replacement case a surgeon who performs that procedure. Many states also require that a standard-of-care expert practice in the same specialty as the defendant, so confirm your jurisdiction's rule.

### Can an orthopedic surgeon testify about future surgery and damages?

Yes. In personal injury cases, an orthopedic surgeon commonly testifies about the need for future surgery, the permanency of the injury, work restrictions, and the reasonableness of treatment, all of which support the damages model. This testimony often works alongside a life care planner and an economist.

### What is the difference between an orthopedic expert in a malpractice case and a PI case?

In a malpractice case the orthopedic surgeon is primarily a standard-of-care expert, establishing what should have been done and whether the defendant breached it. In a PI case the surgeon is primarily a causation and damages expert, addressing whether the accident caused the injury and what future care it requires. The same surgeon can sometimes do both, but the focus differs.

### How early should I retain an orthopedic expert?

Early, especially in medical malpractice cases where same-specialty and active-practice requirements narrow the available pool. Starting early gives you time to find a conflict-free expert in the exact subspecialty and to coordinate record review or an independent medical examination with your case deadlines.

## The Bottom Line

An orthopedic surgery expert witness serves two of the most common case types in litigation: medical malpractice, where the surgeon establishes standard of care and causation, and personal injury, where the surgeon addresses causation, permanency, and future care. The keys are matching the surgeon to the exact subspecialty and body part at issue, confirming board certification and active practice, satisfying any same-specialty requirement in malpractice cases, and starting early enough to find a qualified, conflict-free expert in a sometimes narrow pool.
