Electricity does not leave obvious witnesses. When someone is electrocuted, a panel catches fire, or a piece of equipment fails and injures a worker, the cause is usually buried in code violations, design decisions, and physical evidence that a jury cannot interpret on its own. That is where an electrical engineering expert witness becomes the difference between a case that settles well and a case that stalls. This guide covers when attorneys need one, what they can testify to, the credentials that actually matter, and how to match the right engineer to the posture of your case.

## When You Need an Electrical Engineering Expert Witness

Attorneys do not always know the technical name for the expert they need, and they search for these professionals under a lot of different labels. You might be looking for an electrical accident expert, a faulty wiring expert witness, an electrocution expert witness, an electrical fire expert, a power line accident expert, or simply someone who can prove a defective electrical product caused an injury. Many attorneys search for an electrician expert witness, though for testimony you almost always want a licensed electrical engineer rather than a working electrician, for reasons covered below. All of these point to the same category of expert: an electrical engineer who can analyze an electrical failure and explain it to a jury.

Most attorneys reach for an electrical engineer in a handful of recurring situations.

Electrocution and shock injury cases are the most common. When a worker, tenant, or bystander is injured or killed by contact with energized equipment, an electrical engineering expert can reconstruct how the current path formed, whether proper grounding and bonding were in place, and whether the responsible party followed the National Electrical Code. These cases often turn on whether a ground fault circuit interrupter should have been installed and functioning.

Electrical fire origin and cause is another major category. Many structure fires and equipment fires trace back to faulty wiring, overloaded circuits, failed connections, or defective components. An electrical engineer works alongside or in place of a fire investigator to determine whether an electrical fault was the ignition source, and whether a design or installation defect caused it.

Product liability claims involving electrical or electronic products frequently need an engineer to establish a defect. Failed chargers, batteries, appliances, power tools, and industrial equipment all fall here. The expert evaluates whether the product was defectively designed, defectively manufactured, or failed to carry adequate warnings.

Workplace and industrial accidents involving arc flash, equipment failure, lockout/tagout failures, or machine safety often require an electrical engineer to opine on standard of care and OSHA compliance.

Finally, electrical engineers appear in construction defect, utility, and premises liability matters where wiring, panels, or electrical systems were installed or maintained improperly.

## What an Electrical Engineering Expert Witness Can Testify To

The scope is broad, which is why matching the engineer's actual background to your specific issue matters so much. Common areas of opinion include:

Code compliance and standard of care, primarily against the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70), NFPA 70E for workplace electrical safety, and applicable local and state amendments. An engineer can establish what a reasonable installer, designer, or maintainer should have done.

Electrocution and shock causation, including current path analysis, grounding and bonding adequacy, GFCI presence and function, and whether the injury was consistent with the alleged contact.

Electrical fire origin and cause, including arc fault analysis, evidence of overheating at connections, conductor failure, and whether an electrical event preceded or resulted from the fire.

Arc flash and arc blast analysis, including incident energy calculations, boundary determinations, and whether proper personal protective equipment and labeling were in place.

Product defect, including design defect, manufacturing defect, and failure analysis on the component or system that failed.

Accident reconstruction for electrical events, tying physical evidence to a coherent sequence of how the failure and injury occurred.

Damages-adjacent opinions in some matters, such as the cost to repair or remediate a defective electrical system.

## The Credentials That Matter

Not every electrical engineer is the right fit, and the credential set tells you a lot about whether an expert will survive a Daubert or Frye challenge.

A Professional Engineer (PE) license is close to non-negotiable for testimony. It signals that the engineer is legally qualified to practice and take responsibility for engineering judgments. For most electrical matters, you want a PE with the electrical discipline specifically. This is also why a licensed electrical engineer beats a working electrician for testimony. An electrician installs and repairs systems and can be a useful fact witness, but an electrical engineer has the analytical and code background to render expert opinions that survive a Daubert challenge. If you searched for an electrician expert witness, this is the distinction to keep in mind.

A degree in electrical engineering, ideally with graduate work or decades of applied practice in the relevant subfield, matters because electrical engineering is wide. The person who designs power distribution systems is not necessarily the right witness for a consumer electronics defect.

Subject-matter alignment is the part attorneys most often get wrong. An expert who has spent a career in industrial power systems is ideal for an arc flash or panel fire case but may be a weak fit for a lithium battery failure in a consumer product. Match the engineer's real-world experience to the specific failure mode in your case.

For fire-related electrical cases, look for engineers who also hold or work closely with fire investigation certifications, since electrical fire causation sits at the intersection of both disciplines and NFPA 921 will be in play.

A testimony record matters for admissibility and for knowing how the expert holds up. Ask about prior deposition and trial experience, whether they have been excluded under Daubert, and whether they testify for both plaintiff and defense, which signals objectivity to a jury.

## Matching the Expert to Your Case Posture

A defense electrical expert and a plaintiff electrical expert do the same analysis, but the assignment differs. On the plaintiff side, the engineer establishes the defect or code violation and ties it to causation and injury. On the defense side, the engineer often critiques the opposing expert's methodology, offers alternative causation, or shows that the standard of care was met. Many of the strongest electrical engineering experts work both sides, and that balance is an asset when their objectivity is challenged on cross.

Timing matters more in electrical cases than most. Physical evidence, the failed panel, the burned appliance, the damaged conductor, degrades and can be lost or spoliated. Engaging an engineer early enough to inspect and preserve evidence before it disappears can decide the case. Do not wait until expert disclosure deadlines to start the search.

## Common Questions Attorneys Ask

### Do I need an electrical engineer or an electrician?

For expert testimony, you almost always want a licensed electrical engineer, not a working electrician. An electrician installs and maintains systems and can serve as a fact witness about what they saw or did, but an electrical engineer has the engineering degree, the PE license, and the code analysis background to render opinions that hold up under a Daubert challenge. If your case needs someone to explain why a system failed and tie it to a standard of care, that is an engineer's role.

### What kind of expert do I need for an electrical fire?

An electrical fire usually calls for an electrical engineer, often working alongside a fire investigator. The fire investigator establishes origin and cause under NFPA 921, and the electrical engineer determines whether an electrical fault, faulty wiring, an overloaded circuit, or a defective component was the ignition source. In cases where the dispute is specifically about the electrical system, an electrical engineer with fire experience can cover both angles.

### Who can testify in an electrocution or electrical shock case?

An electrical engineering expert reconstructs how the current path formed, whether grounding and bonding were adequate, whether a GFCI should have been present and functioning, and whether the responsible party met the National Electrical Code. This applies whether the injured person was a worker, a tenant, or a bystander, and whether the contact was with wiring, equipment, or a power line.

### What expert proves an electrical product was defective?

Product liability claims involving electrical or electronic products need an electrical engineer who can perform failure analysis and establish whether the product was defectively designed, defectively manufactured, or sold without adequate warnings. This covers failed batteries, chargers, appliances, power tools, and industrial equipment.

### How quickly should I retain an electrical engineering expert?

As early as possible. Electrical cases hinge on physical evidence, the failed panel, the burned appliance, the damaged conductor, and that evidence degrades or gets spoliated over time. Retaining an engineer early enough to inspect and preserve the evidence often decides the case, so do not wait until the disclosure deadline.
